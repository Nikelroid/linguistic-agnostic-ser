\title{Sparse-Autoencoder Dissection of Emotion Features in Frozen Speech Encoders}
\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\affiliation{\institution{Anonymous}\country{}}
\renewcommand{\shortauthors}{Anonymous}

\begin{abstract}
Linear probes show that emotion is decodable in the middle-to-late layers of frozen speech transformers, but not \emph{which} internal features carry it, nor whether the signal a probe exploits is acoustic or lexical. We train TopK Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) on the \emph{per-frame} activations of six frozen large encoders (HuBERT, WavLM, wav2vec2, w2v-BERT, MERT, Whisper) and dissect their emotion features on the lexicon-controlled CREMA-D corpus (91 actors $\times$ 12 fixed sentences $\times$ 6 emotions, 7{,}442 clips). A \emph{confound-controlled} selection---a feature's firing must be more informative about emotion than about the spoken sentence or the speaker---isolates genuine emotion features from phonetic and speaker-driven ones, cutting 394 naively-selected features to 61 clean ones. We report four results: (i) emotion \emph{disentangles} into clean monosemantic features earlier (L2--L7) than where it is best linearly \emph{decoded} (L10); (ii) all six encoders share an acoustic emotion-feature basis that generalizes across unseen speakers (leave-speaker-out drop $\approx0$); (iii) the recovered signal is acoustic, not lexical (decodes at 0.55 from acoustic vs.\ 0.43 from lexical features); and (iv) on the EMIS incongruent-speech benchmark the lexical shortcut is a \emph{deep-layer} phenomenon that only the ASR-trained SSL encoders develop, while Whisper stays audio-grounded at every layer. Decodability and disentanglement are thus distinct axes, and emotion here is carried by speaker-invariant acoustic features.
\end{abstract}
\keywords{speech emotion recognition, sparse autoencoders, mechanistic interpretability, probing, disentanglement, acoustic features}

\section{Introduction}
Self-supervised speech encoders dominate speech emotion recognition (SER), yet were pretrained on ASR-style objectives that encode substantial linguistic structure. Layer-wise probing tells us \emph{where} emotion is readable, but not \emph{which} features encode it, whether the depth pattern holds across encoders and speakers, or whether the signal a probe exploits is acoustic (how something is said) or lexical (what is said). These distinctions matter: a model that reads emotion off lexical content will fail under sarcasm, accented speech, and low-resource languages where prosody and meaning diverge.

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) decompose dense activations into an over-complete dictionary of sparse, near-monosemantic features~\cite{gao2024scaling,saelens}, turning these questions from decodability into mechanism. A known risk is that an SAE trained on generic speech rediscovers \emph{phonemes} rather than affect~\cite{saeasr2026,pluth2026asrsae}; we neutralize it by anchoring on CREMA-D~\cite{cao2014cremad}, whose 12 fixed carrier sentences hold text constant across emotions, so any recovered emotion signal there is necessarily acoustic and provides a built-in positive control.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} (1) A confound-controlled emotion-feature definition for speech SAEs that exploits a lexicon-controlled factorial corpus to separate affect from phonetic and speaker content; (2) evidence that \emph{disentanglement} and \emph{decodability} peak at different depths; (3) an acoustic-vs-lexical labeling of features with a causal sufficiency test; and (4) a depth-resolved account of the EMIS lexical shortcut showing it is confined to the deep layers of ASR-trained SSL encoders. All code, activations, and artifacts are released.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{SAEs for speech/audio models.} The closest prior work is \emph{AudioSAE}~\cite{audiosae2026} (EACL 2026), which trains the same method family---BatchTop-K SAEs, $8\times$ expansion, per-frame activations from every encoder layer---on frozen Whisper-small and HuBERT-base, and even measures cross-model feature universality (same-layer/different-seed coverage $>$50\%; HuBERT and Whisper features do not align). However, it treats emotion only as one of four generic downstream probe tasks (gender, noise, accent, emotion), with \emph{no} monosemantic emotion-feature interpretation, no confound control, no lexicon-controlled corpus, no acoustic-vs-lexical separation, and only two encoders. \cite{pluth2026asrsae} apply a TopK SAE but only at Whisper's \emph{final} encoder layer (no depth analysis, listed as future work) and discover linguistic/acoustic features with no affect target. \cite{mariotte2026audioexplain} frame SAEs as going beyond linear probing for audio foundation models (AST/HuBERT/WavLM/MERT), but their case study is singing-technique classification, not SER.

\noindent\textbf{Mechanistic SER and the lexical shortcut.} \cite{ma2026lora} analyze LoRA-adapted Whisper for SER with layer-contribution probing, logit-lens, SVD, and CKA---but explicitly without SAEs. The acoustic-vs-lexical question is studied with text-dependency analyses~\cite{textdep2024,lexicalser2025} and the EMIS incongruent-speech benchmark~\cite{emis2025}. Our work is the first to combine SAE feature dissection with confound control on a factorial corpus, and to localize the EMIS lexical shortcut to depth.

\section{Method}
\textbf{Per-frame activations.} The project's cached embeddings are mean-pooled per utterance---too few to fit an $8\times$ over-complete SAE---so we re-extract \emph{un-pooled} per-frame hidden states from the six frozen large encoders (HuBERT~\cite{hsu2021hubert}, WavLM, wav2vec2, w2v-BERT, MERT, Whisper~\cite{radford2023whisper}): 25 states per frame (CNN front-end L0 + 24 transformer layers), with Whisper's padding frames masked. CREMA-D layer~13 alone yields $940{,}273$ frames.

\noindent\textbf{TopK SAE.} We train a TopK SAE ($d_{\text{in}}{=}1024$, $d_{\text{sae}}{=}8192$, $k{=}32$ active features) with a unit-norm decoder, input standardisation, and an AuxK auxiliary loss that revives dead features. It reconstructs well: fraction of variance unexplained (FVU) $=0.070$ ($93\%$ variance explained) with only $4/8192$ dead features.

\noindent\textbf{Confound-controlled emotion features.} We score each feature by the mutual information (MI) between its firing indicator and the emotion label, thresholded at $5\sigma$ over a label-permutation null. A feature qualifies as an \emph{emotion feature} only if (a) it is significant, (b) it is monosemantic ($\geq0.5$ activation mass on one emotion), and (c) $\mathrm{MI}(\text{emotion})$ exceeds both $\mathrm{MI}(\text{sentence})$ and $\mathrm{MI}(\text{actor})$ on CREMA-D's factorial. Significance and monosemanticity alone flag 394 features, but $\sim$66\% of those carry more information about the spoken sentence (phonetic content) or the speaker than about emotion---the documented ``SAE rediscovers phonemes, not affect'' failure mode. Confound control leaves 61 genuine emotion features (median monosemanticity 0.59), dominated by anger and sadness; fear and neutral fall to zero, consistent with acoustic encoding of high-arousal, acoustically-marked emotions.

\noindent\textbf{Acoustic vs.\ lexical labeling.} For each feature we compare the cross-validated ROC-AUC of a logistic classifier predicting its firing from 88-dim eGeMAPS descriptors~\cite{eyben2016egemaps} (acoustic) versus the carrier-sentence identity (lexical); $R^2$ is unusable because the pooled features are $\sim$96\% zero.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Depth: decode $\neq$ disentangle (Q\,i)}
A 25-layer sweep separates two curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:summary} top-left; Table~\ref{tab:key}). Probe accuracy peaks at L10 (0.739) and stays high into the deepest layers, but the \emph{count} of monosemantic emotion features peaks earlier at L7, and monosemanticity at L2. The deep layers L17--24 retain decodability ($\sim$0.72) yet collapse to 24--47 emotion features: emotion stays linearly readable while becoming distributed rather than packed into clean directions. It organises into interpretable features \emph{before} the depth at which a probe reads it best.

\subsection{Across paradigms and speakers (Q\,ii)}
All six encoders carry emotion features at their best layer (HuBERT 61, wav2vec2 70, Whisper 79, WavLM 145, MERT 64, w2v-BERT 61), each anger/sadness-dominated. Matching features by their per-utterance firing correlation, the Whisper$\rightarrow$SSL overlap (0.40) essentially equals the SSL$\rightarrow$SSL overlap (0.39): on fixed-lexicon CREMA-D Whisper is \emph{not} distinct, so its previously reported divergence concerns the lexical shortcut, not acoustic emotion encoding. Under speaker-disjoint cross-validation over the 91 actors emotion accuracy is unchanged (random 0.551 vs.\ disjoint 0.551; leave-speaker-out drop $\approx0$) with $\sim$95\% actor coverage. Because confound control already removed speaker-entangled features, the published MESD leave-one-speaker-out collapse ($\sim$0.30) is attributable to exactly those excluded features.

\subsection{Acoustic, not lexical (Q\,iii)}
The positive control (fixed lexicon $\Rightarrow$ acoustic) labels 60/61 emotion features acoustic, with mean eGeMAPS firing-AUC 0.83 versus sentence AUC 0.66. Causally (Fig.~\ref{fig:summary} bottom-right), emotion decodes from the 61 acoustic features at 0.552, versus 0.431 from 61 lexical and 0.423 from 61 random features (6-class; full reconstruction 0.73). Since the signal is distributed across thousands of features, necessity-ablation of any small subset is near-null and \emph{sufficiency} is the informative test: the recovered emotion signal is acoustic.

\subsection{EMIS: the lexical shortcut is a deep-layer phenomenon (Q\,iv)}
On EMIS~\cite{emis2025} (1{,}248 clips with text and prosodic emotion set independently) we train an emotion probe on congruent clips and test on incongruent ones; text-bias $=$ text accuracy $-$ audio accuracy (positive $\Rightarrow$ lexical shortcut). On raw mean-pooled activations every encoder is audio-grounded in its early/mid layers; the shortcut emerges only in the \emph{deep} layers of the ASR-trained SSL encoders (HuBERT L20 $+0.79$, WavLM L20 $+0.73$, wav2vec2 $+0.87$), whereas Whisper stays audio-grounded at every depth (L24 $-0.11$) and the music model MERT and w2v-BERT never take it (Fig.~\ref{fig:emis}). This reproduces the published per-encoder text-bias (HuBERT $+0.72$ $\dots$ Whisper $-0.19$) and localizes it to depth. A methodological caution: probing the SAE \emph{reconstruction} spuriously inverted Whisper's text-bias, so the layer sweep is run on raw activations.

\section{Discussion}
Decodability and disentanglement are distinct axes. A probe reads emotion best where the representation is linearly separable (L10 and deep layers), but the encoder packs emotion into clean monosemantic features earlier (L2--L7); deep layers trade interpretable structure for distributed separability. This sharpens the prior ``middle-to-late'' decodability result into a claim about where emotion is actually \emph{organised}. On lexicon-controlled data all six encoders converge on a shared acoustic basis whose features survive speaker-disjoint evaluation; the Whisper-vs-SSL divergence~\cite{ma2026lora} concerns the lexical shortcut, which CREMA-D cannot pose and the EMIS experiment does---confirming the prior text-bias result and adding that the shortcut lives at a depth Whisper never reaches.

\section{Limitations}
Utterance-pooled sparse features make necessity-ablation of small subsets near-null (the signal is distributed), so we rely on sufficiency. The SAE is the right tool for the per-feature CREMA-D analyses but \emph{not} as a stand-in for raw activations in the EMIS probe, where reconstruction inverted Whisper. Findings are demonstrated primarily on HuBERT/CREMA-D; the cross-encoder and EMIS arms use one layer per encoder rather than full 25-layer SAE grids for all six. The acoustic axis is eGeMAPS; richer acoustic descriptors or learned prosody features could refine the labeling.

\section{Future Work}
Two arms are stubbed and await data: an IEMOCAP real-transcript causal ablation (transcripts not yet synced) and a SpeechCraft cross-lingual feature-overlap study (audio not on the cluster) to explain the published EN$\leftrightarrow$ZH collapse as disjoint feature sets. Beyond these: full 25-layer SAE grids for all six encoders; steering experiments (activating an emotion feature to causally shift predictions); and extending the confound-controlled definition to dimensional V/A/D targets.

\section{Positioning and Publishability}
Method-wise we overlap AudioSAE~\cite{audiosae2026} (same SAE family, per-frame, all layers, cross-model universality), but our contribution is orthogonal in substance: confound-controlled \emph{emotion}-feature identification on a lexicon-controlled factorial corpus across six encoders, the decodability$\neq$disentanglement-by-depth finding, and the depth-localized EMIS lexical shortcut---none of which AudioSAE, the ASR-SAE works~\cite{pluth2026asrsae,mariotte2026audioexplain}, or the non-SAE mechanistic SER work~\cite{ma2026lora} provide. The combination is, to our knowledge, un-scooped. A workshop or short-paper venue at an affective-computing or interpretability track is a realistic target, framing AudioSAE as concurrent method work and our affect-specific, confound-controlled analysis as the new contribution.
