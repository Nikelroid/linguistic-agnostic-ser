\title{Sparse-Autoencoder Dissection of Emotion Features in Frozen Speech Encoders}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\affiliation{\institution{Anonymous}\country{}}
\renewcommand{\shortauthors}{Anonymous}

\begin{abstract}
Linear probes show emotion is decodable in the middle-to-late layers of frozen speech transformers, but not which internal features carry it, nor whether the signal is acoustic or lexical. We train TopK Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) on \emph{per-frame} activations of six frozen large encoders and dissect their emotion features on the lexicon-controlled CREMA-D corpus (91 actors $\times$ 12 fixed sentences $\times$ 6 emotions). A confound-controlled selection---a feature's firing must be more informative about emotion than about the spoken sentence or the speaker---isolates genuine emotion features from phonetic and speaker-driven ones. We find that (i) emotion \emph{disentangles} into clean monosemantic features earlier (L2--L7) than where it is best linearly \emph{decoded} (L10); (ii) all six encoders share an acoustic emotion-feature basis that generalizes across unseen speakers; and (iii) the recovered emotion signal is acoustic, not lexical. Decodability and disentanglement are thus distinct, and emotion here is carried by speaker-invariant acoustic features.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{speech emotion recognition, sparse autoencoders, mechanistic interpretability, probing, acoustic features, disentanglement}

\section{Introduction}
Self-supervised speech encoders dominate speech emotion recognition (SER), yet were trained on ASR-style objectives that encode substantial linguistic structure. Layer-wise probing answers \emph{where} emotion is readable, but cannot say \emph{which} features encode it, whether the depth pattern holds across encoders, speakers and languages, or whether the signal a probe exploits is acoustic (how something is said) or lexical (what is said). Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) decompose dense activations into an over-complete dictionary of sparse, near-monosemantic features~\cite{gao2024scaling,saelens}, turning these questions from decodability into mechanism. Because SAEs trained on generic speech tend to rediscover \emph{phonemes} rather than affect~\cite{saeasr2026}, we anchor the study on CREMA-D~\cite{cao2014cremad}, whose 12 fixed carrier sentences hold text constant across emotions: any emotion signal recovered there is necessarily acoustic, giving a built-in positive control. We contribute (1) a confound-controlled emotion-feature definition for speech SAEs, and a three-part answer showing that (2) disentanglement and decodability peak at different depths, and (3) the emotion features are acoustic and speaker-invariant.

\section{Method}
\textbf{Per-frame activations.}
The cached embeddings are mean-pooled per utterance---too few to fit an $8\times$ over-complete SAE---so we re-extract \emph{un-pooled} per-frame hidden states from the six frozen large encoders (HuBERT~\cite{hsu2021hubert}, WavLM, wav2vec2, w2v-BERT, MERT, Whisper~\cite{radford2023whisper}): 25 states per frame (CNN L0 + 24 transformer layers), Whisper padding masked. CREMA-D layer~13 alone gives $940{,}273$ frames. We train a TopK SAE ($d_{\text{in}}{=}1024$, $d_{\text{sae}}{=}8192$, $k{=}32$) with unit-norm decoder, input standardisation, and an AuxK loss reviving dead features; it reaches FVU~$=0.070$ ($93\%$ variance explained) with $4/8192$ dead features.

\noindent\textbf{Confound-controlled emotion features.}
We score each feature by the mutual information (MI) between its firing and the emotion label, thresholded at $5\sigma$ over a label-permutation null. Significance plus monosemanticity ($\geq0.5$ mass on one emotion) flags $394$ features---but $\sim$66\% carry \emph{more} information about the spoken sentence (phonetic content) or the speaker than about emotion. Requiring $\mathrm{MI}(\text{emotion})>\mathrm{MI}(\text{sentence})$ \emph{and} $>\mathrm{MI}(\text{actor})$ removes them, leaving $61$ genuine emotion features (median monosemanticity $0.59$), dominated by anger and sadness; fear and neutral fall to zero, consistent with acoustic encoding of high-arousal emotions. For each, we compare the cross-validated ROC-AUC of a logistic classifier predicting its firing from eGeMAPS descriptors~\cite{eyben2016egemaps} (acoustic) vs.\ carrier-sentence identity (lexical); $R^2$ is unusable as the features are $\sim$96\% zero.

\subsection{Depth: decode $\neq$ disentangle (Q\,i)}
A 25-layer sweep separates two curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:summary}, top-left; Table~\ref{tab:key}). Probe accuracy peaks at L10 and stays high into the deepest layers, but the \emph{count} of monosemantic emotion features peaks earlier at L7 (and monosemanticity at L2). The deep layers L17--24 retain decodability ($\sim$0.72) yet collapse to 24--47 emotion features: emotion stays linearly readable while becoming distributed rather than packed into clean directions. \emph{It organises into interpretable features before the depth at which a probe reads it best.}

\subsection{Across paradigms and speakers (Q\,ii)}
All six encoders carry emotion features at their best layer (Fig.~\ref{fig:summary}, bottom-left), each anger/sadness-dominated. Matching features by their per-utterance firing correlation, the Whisper$\rightarrow$SSL overlap ($0.40$) equals the SSL$\rightarrow$SSL overlap ($0.39$): on fixed-lexicon CREMA-D Whisper is \emph{not} distinct, so its known divergence concerns the lexical shortcut, not acoustic emotion encoding. Under speaker-disjoint cross-validation across the 91 actors emotion accuracy is unchanged (leave-speaker-out drop $\approx0$; $\sim$95\% actor coverage); confound control already removed the speaker-entangled features behind the published MESD leave-one-speaker-out collapse ($\sim$0.30).

\subsection{Acoustic, not lexical (Q\,iii)}
The positive control (fixed lexicon $\Rightarrow$ acoustic) labels $60/61$ emotion features acoustic (eGeMAPS firing-AUC $0.83$ vs.\ sentence $0.66$). Causally (Fig.~\ref{fig:summary}, bottom-right), emotion decodes from the $61$ acoustic features at $0.552$, vs.\ $0.431$ from lexical and $0.423$ from random features (6-class; full reconstruction $0.73$). Since the signal is distributed, sufficiency rather than ablation is the informative test: the recovered emotion signal is acoustic.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Decodability and disentanglement are distinct axes: a probe reads emotion best where the representation is linearly separable (L10 and the deep layers), but the encoder packs emotion into clean monosemantic features earlier (L2--L7), sharpening the prior ``middle-to-late'' decodability result into a claim about where emotion is organised. On lexicon-controlled data all six encoders converge on a shared acoustic basis whose features survive speaker-disjoint evaluation; the Whisper-vs-SSL divergence concerns the lexical shortcut~\cite{ma2026lora}, which CREMA-D cannot pose and the deferred EMIS test targets. Three experiments await cluster data, each with a runnable stub: the IEMOCAP real-transcript ablation, the SpeechCraft cross-lingual arm, and the EMIS audio-vs-text separation. All artifacts---activations, SAEs, per-step CSVs/plots/metrics, and this paper---are released for reproduction.
